Classification of Data
Nominal: categorical without a natural order 
   (e.g., gender, ethnicity, eye)
Ordinal: categorical with a natural order 
   (e.g., socio-economic status, parity)
Discrete: countable, ordered numerical data 
   (e.g., IQ)
Continuous: ordered numerical data that can theoretically take on any value 
   (e.g., height, weight, age)

Types of Data
To analyze data sets that contain nominal variables we typically assign a number to each category
For blood type we may assign a 0 for type A, a 1 for type B, a 2 for type O, and 3 for AB.
The choices of the numbers 0,1,2 and 3 are arbitrary.
We could have alternatively picked 71, 34, -785, and 34,956.

=== SLIDE 9 ===
Statistical Inference
Making an assertion about the population (true) parameter on the basis of a sample statistic, subject to sampling error.

Parameter: a characteristic of a variable in the population (Greek symbols usually used)--average, proportion, correlation, etc.

Statistic: an estimate of a population parameter based on a sample (Latin letters)

=== SLIDE 10 ===
Data Collection
A representative sample is one in which the distribution of values in the sample mimics the distribution of values in the population.
For instance, a representative sample of size 100 people from a population made of one million people would be such that the histogram of the 100 observations and the histogram of the 1,000,000 observations are similar.
If a sample is representative, then characteristics of the sample will be similar to characteristics in the population.

=== SLIDE 11 ===
Data Collection
Alternatively, if the sample is not representative, then characteristics of the sample could be very misleading because they could be very different from the true population characteristics.
Sampling bias or large non-response
There is a huge literature on the issue of how to select a sample.
The one commonality of all sampling schemes is that they are designed to produce a sample that is likely to be representative of the population of interest.

=== SLIDE 13 ===
Simple random sample (SRS)
The simple random sample (SRS) is the most basic of the sampling designs and is the staple of survey sampling.
For an SRS, every group of size n has the same chance of being selected.
Note that the process of actually obtaining an SRS can be very simple (e.g. an SRS of students in this class) or can be very difficult if not impossible (e.g. an SRS of eagles in California).

=== SLIDE 14 ===
Stratified random sample
A stratified random sample is conducted by separating the population into k strata and then selecting an SRS from within each strata.
Presidential polling (with, e.g., k = 4) often involve stratified random samples.

=== SLIDE 15 ===
Cluster sampling
Cluster sampling is conducted by dividing the population into distinct clusters.
Then obtain an SRS of clusters and collect data from everyone in the selected clusters.
Monthly census surveys use cluster sampling of households to gather data.

=== SLIDE 22 ===
Empirical Rule
When data are symmetric and unimodal:
Approximately 68% of the observations fall within one standard deviation of the mean
Approximately 95% fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean
Approximately all the data fall within 3 standard deviation of the mean.

=== SLIDE 23 ===
Percentiles
The sample median is the 50th percentile.
Like the mean and median, percentiles are conceptually simple to think about.
For example, the 75th percentile is a number such that 75% of the observations in a data set are below.
The 25th percentile is a number such that 25% of the observations are below.
Students whose grades for this course are above the 95th percentile would be happy
While students with grades below the 10th percentile might not be happy.

Histograms
A histogram displays the frequency distribution of continuous or discrete data.
To construct a histogram we partition the range of our data into distinct groups called bins.
For example, for the dose response lifetime data above we could create bins defined by
(0, 100), (101, 200), (201, 300), (301, 400) and so on up to (701, 800)

=== SLIDE 29 ===
Histograms
Then we count the number of observations falling into each bin
Plot a vertical bar centered at the bin midpoint whose height is equal to the count for that bin.
This creates a frequency histogram.
To create a relative frequency histogram simply divide each bin count by the sample size.

=== SLIDE 32 ===
Boxplots
A boxplot is another graph that is often used to display data.
Side-by-side boxplots can be used to efficiently and clearly compare different groups.
Unfortunately there are several methods for constructing boxplots.
But the main ingredients are as follows.
The “box” is constructed using the 25th, 50th (i.e. median), and 75th percentiles of the data set.
These are denoted Q1, Q2, and Q3

=== SLIDE 33 ===
Boxplots
The “whiskers” extend above and below the box.
The length of these whiskers varies depending on the definition used.
We will extend to the minimum and maximum values in the data set.
In this case the boxplot is constructed using	Minimum Q1 (the first quartile) Q2 (the median) Q3 (the third quartile) Maximum
