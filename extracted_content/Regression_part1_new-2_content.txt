=== SLIDE 29 ===
Assessing the Assumptions of Linear Regression
Five assumptions for linear regression:
Existence: 
Response variable has variability for any fixed explanatory variable.
Key for estimating population mean and variance in regression.
Example: Stopping distance varies for each vehicle speed, satisfying this assumption.

=== SLIDE 30 ===
Assessing the Assumptions of Linear Regression
Five assumptions for linear regression:
2. Independence:
Observations are independent given the explanatory variable.
Typically met with one measurement per subject
Example: Stopping distances for different vehicles are independent.
Violation Example: 
visual acuity; there are 2 eyes per person.
Height measured multiple times on each subject over time.
cholesterol; measured on each study subject and their family members
Violations can lead to underestimated standard errors and inaccurate confidence intervals.
Solutions: Use models like GEE, Mixed Models, or clustering methods to account for dependence.

=== SLIDE 31 ===
Assessing the Assumptions of Linear Regression
Five assumptions for linear regression:
3. Linearity:
Mean response variable is a linear function of the predictor variable.


Implies a proportional relationship: changes in predictor lead to consistent changes in response.
Example: Stopping distance changes linearly with vehicle speed.
Assessed by examining scatter plots for a linear trend.
Well-fitting models show data points scattered around a straight regression line.

=== SLIDE 35 ===
Assessing the Assumptions of Linear Regression
Five assumptions for linear regression:
4. Homoscedasticity:
The variance of the response variable is constant across all levels of the predictor variable.

Implies that the spread of residuals does not change as the predictor variable changes.
Example: Stopping distance variance remains consistent across different vehicle speeds.
Heteroscedasticity affects the precision of standard errors, leading to inaccurate hypothesis tests and confidence intervals.
Diagnosed by plotting residuals vs. predicted values—random scatter indicates homoscedasticity; patterns suggest violations.

=== SLIDE 38 ===
Assessing the Assumptions of Linear Regression
Five assumptions for linear regression:
5. Normality: Are the residuals normally distributed?
Residuals should be normally distributed for any predictor value.
Critical for valid hypothesis tests and confidence intervals.
Violations affect tests and intervals, not coefficients.
With large samples, normality is less crucial
Minor deviations from normality are acceptable with large samples.

=== SLIDE 42 ===
Summary
Key Components:
 Intercept (β₀): Value of Y when X = 0.
 Slope (β₁): Change in Y for a one-unit increase in X.
 R²: Percentage of variance in Y explained by X.
 F-Statistic: Tests the overall model significance.
Centering X: Shifts X to make the intercept meaningful (mean value of Y when X is at its average).
Predictions:
Confidence Interval: Range for the true mean response.
Prediction Interval: Range for a single future observation.
Assumptions:
Existence: Variability in Y across X values.
Independence: Observations are independent.
Linearity: Y is a linear function of X.
Homoscedasticity: Constant variance of Y.
Normality: Residuals are normally distributed.
